FORMAT MATCHING EXAMPLES
"I know the steps but people need a clear sequence." -> A guide or short course might fit.
"People repeatedly build the same thing from scratch." -> A template, worksheet, spreadsheet, or resource pack might fit.
"People need examples they can adapt." -> A swipe file, asset pack, or template collection might fit.
"People need a task performed automatically." -> A software/tool concept may fit.

These are prompts for exploration, not rules. One problem can support several formats.

BEFORE YOU CHOOSE AN IDEA
You do not need to start with a topic you are "passionate" about. Useful product ideas can come from work you already know, a problem you solved for yourself, a process you repeat, a resource you wish had existed, or something other people regularly ask you to explain.

The strongest clue is not necessarily excitement. It is often a problem you understand well enough to imagine helping someone solve.

START WITH THE RESULT, THEN CHOOSE THE FORMAT

A common beginner mistake is choosing a format before choosing what the product is supposed to accomplish. "I want to make an ebook" sounds specific, but it still leaves the important question unanswered: what would someone buy the ebook to learn, solve, decide, create, or do?

The same useful information might become a guide, workbook, spreadsheet, template pack, short course, reference library, calculator, or simple software tool. The best format depends on what the customer needs to do with it.

If the customer needs an explanation, a guide may be enough. If they need to repeat a process, a template or checklist may be more useful. If they need to calculate or organize information, a spreadsheet or tool may fit better. If they need demonstration and practice, a course may make more sense.

You are not trying to choose the most impressive format. You are trying to choose the smallest format that delivers the result well.
